Most people study by rereading. It feels productive because the material gets easier to read each
time. That fluency is the trap: recognising text is not the same as being able to produce it.

## Retrieval beats review

The **testing effect** is one of the most replicated results in cognitive psychology. Trying to
recall something strengthens the memory more than reviewing it does — even when the recall attempt
fails.

Practically, that means closing the book and writing down what you remember is worth more than
another pass through the highlights.

<Callout type="note" title="Why it feels worse">
Retrieval is effortful and you get immediate evidence of what you do not know. Rereading is smooth
and flattering. The uncomfortable method is the one that works.
</Callout>

## Spacing beats cramming

Given a fixed number of study minutes, spreading them across several days produces far better
long-term retention than spending them all at once. The effect is large and holds across ages and
subjects.

A workable schedule for something you want to keep:

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Same day |
| 2nd | 2 days later |
| 3rd | 1 week later |
| 4th | 3 weeks later |
| 5th | 2 months later |

Each successful recall lets you push the next review further out.

## Putting them together

Spacing and retrieval compound. The practical form is a question you answer from memory, scheduled
at increasing intervals — which is exactly what flashcard software automates.

Writing good cards matters more than the tool:

- **One idea per card.** If the answer has three parts, make three cards.
- **Ask for production, not recognition.** "What does `minmax(0, 1fr)` solve?" beats "Is
  `minmax(0, 1fr)` useful? Yes/No."
- **Include context.** A card you cannot understand in six months is a card you will delete.

<Callout type="warning" title="Cards are not a substitute for understanding">
Memorising steps you cannot explain produces brittle knowledge. Understand first, then use spacing
to keep what you understood.
</Callout>

## Interleaving

A related finding: mixing problem types within a session beats doing twenty of the same type in a
row. Blocked practice lets you apply a method on autopilot; interleaved practice forces you to first
decide *which* method applies, which is the skill you actually need later.

Expect interleaved practice to feel worse and score lower during the session. It reliably scores
higher on a delayed test.
