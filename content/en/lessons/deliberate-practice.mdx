Experience alone does not reliably produce expertise. Studies of clinicians, forecasters, and
traders repeatedly find that years on the job correlate weakly — sometimes not at all — with
accuracy. What separates the people who improve is the structure of their practice.

## The conditions that make practice work

Research on expert performance points to a consistent set of conditions:

1. **A specific target** just beyond current ability, not a vague goal like "get better".
2. **Full attention**, not autopilot repetition.
3. **Immediate, informative feedback** on what went wrong.
4. **Repetition with correction**, not repetition alone.

Ordinary work fails condition 3 most often. You ship something, and the feedback arrives months
later, tangled with a dozen other causes.

## Kind and wicked learning environments

Robin Hogarth's distinction is a useful lens:

- A **kind** environment gives fast, accurate, unambiguous feedback. Chess, sight-reading, and
  typing are kind. Practice reliably produces skill.
- A **wicked** environment gives delayed, noisy, or misleading feedback. Hiring, long-horizon
  investing, and management are wicked. Practice can entrench confident errors.

<Callout type="warning" title="The danger of wicked environments">
In a wicked environment, experience raises confidence faster than accuracy. The correction is
deliberate record-keeping, not more years.
</Callout>

## Making a wicked environment kinder

You cannot always change the feedback delay, but you can change what you record.

**Keep a decision journal.** Before a consequential decision, write down: what you expect to happen,
your confidence as a percentage, and what would tell you that you were wrong. Revisit it when the
outcome lands.

This defeats hindsight bias, which otherwise quietly rewrites your memory of what you predicted and
destroys the feedback signal.

```text
Date:        2026-08-20
Decision:    Ship the new onboarding flow to 100% of users
Expectation: Week-1 activation rises from 34% to >40%
Confidence:  60%
Falsifier:   Activation below 36% after two weeks
Review on:   2026-09-03
```

## Separate outcome from process

A good decision can produce a bad outcome and vice versa. Judging yourself only on outcomes teaches
you to chase noise.

Grade both: was the reasoning sound given what was knowable at the time, *and* what happened? Only
the combination tells you whether to change your approach.

## Practising at the edge

The target should sit where you fail perhaps one attempt in four. Too easy and you learn nothing;
too hard and you get no signal about what almost worked.

For most skills this means deliberately isolating the sub-skill you are worst at, rather than
running the whole task end to end. Musicians drill the four hard bars. The equivalent in most fields
is unglamorous and is exactly why people skip it.
