A percentage is a fraction with the denominator fixed at 100. That is the whole definition. Almost
every mistake people make with percentages comes from losing track of *what the denominator was*.

## The one formula

$$
\text{percentage} = \frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} \times 100\%
$$

If 37 of 200 survey respondents said yes, that is $\frac{37}{200} \times 100\% = 18.5\%$.

Rearranged, the same relationship answers the other two questions you will ever ask:

| You know | You want | Compute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| part, whole | percentage | $\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} \times 100$ |
| percentage, whole | part | $\dfrac{\text{percentage}}{100} \times \text{whole}$ |
| part, percentage | whole | $\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{percentage}} \times 100$ |

<Callout type="tip" title="Percent means per hundred">
Reading "%" out loud as "per hundred" makes most problems solve themselves. "18.5 per hundred
respondents" tells you immediately what the denominator is.
</Callout>

## Percentage change

Change is measured against the **starting** value:

$$
\text{percentage change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%
$$

A price going from 80,000 to 92,000 is

$$
\frac{92{,}000 - 80{,}000}{80{,}000} \times 100\% = 15\%
$$

### Increases and decreases are not symmetric

This trips up almost everyone. Go up 50%, then down 50%, and you do **not** end up where you
started:

$$
100 \xrightarrow{\;+50\%\;} 150 \xrightarrow{\;-50\%\;} 75
$$

The reason is that the second percentage is taken of a different whole. To undo a $p\%$ increase you
need a decrease of $\frac{p}{100+p} \times 100\%$ — to undo $+50\%$ you need $-33.3\%$.

<Callout type="warning" title="A 100% decrease is the end of the road">
Something can increase by 300%, but it can never decrease by more than 100% — that would take it
below nothing. If you see "costs fell by 150%", the writer has made an error.
</Callout>

## Percent versus percentage point

This is the distinction that most often turns a true number into a misleading headline.

If support for a policy moves from 40% to 44%:

- It rose **4 percentage points** — the arithmetic difference.
- It rose **10 percent** — the relative change, $\frac{44-40}{40} \times 100\% = 10\%$.

Both are true. A press release that wants the number to sound big will report the second; one that
wants it to sound small will report the first.

<Callout type="note" title="How to spot it">
Whenever a percentage changes *to* another percentage, ask which of the two is being quoted. If the
article does not say, it is usually the flattering one.
</Callout>

## Proportions and ratios

A **proportion** is the same idea without the ×100: $\frac{37}{200} = 0.185$. Statistics generally
prefers proportions because they combine cleanly under multiplication.

A **ratio** compares two parts to each other rather than a part to a whole. Of 200 people, 37 said
yes and 163 said no — the ratio is $37 : 163$, roughly $1 : 4.4$.

Mixing these up produces a specific, common error: "1 in 5 said yes" (proportion, $\frac{1}{5}$)
means something different from "1 to 5 said yes" (ratio, so $\frac{1}{6}$ of the whole).

## Relative risk needs a base rate

"This doubles your risk" is uninterpretable on its own. Doubling a risk of 1 in 2,000,000 gives 1 in
1,000,000 — still nothing. Doubling a risk of 1 in 20 matters a great deal.

$$
\text{absolute change} = \text{base rate} \times (\text{relative change} - 1)
$$

Always look for the base rate. If it is not given, the claim has not actually told you anything.
